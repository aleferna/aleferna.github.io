title: "The Myths of Humans"
description: "We know we exist, but not how we exist. The problem is we do not understand evolution well enough."

Picture the following: you are the child of a blacksmith in a small city[^1] in Mycenaean Greece, around 1500 BC. Your father has just come home after a long day of forging tools and weapons. He works mainly with bronze, since the technology used to smelt iron[^2] would take another 400 years to reach Greece. It is dusk, and while you help your mother cook the pheasant your father bought at the local market, she tells you stories. These are tales of Greek heroes who defeated horrible monsters, defied the gods, or saved their cities from “barbarian” invasions. It is hard to say precisely which stories were popular at this time — writing had just arrived in mainland Greece[^3], and thus no written record from this period exists. However, it is likely that at least some of them eventually became part of the anthology that we now know as the Odyssey, which would be compiled by Homer 700 years later[^4]. From the Odyssey, we know that the stories your mother tells you at night, called “mythos” in Ancient Greek (meaning “narrative” or “fiction”), often featured monsters that were part human, part animal. Centaurs, chimeras, sirens, the Minotaur, and many other creatures[^5] were used frequently as allegories for emotions and impulses, raising profound questions about what it meant to be human[^6]. Later, the idea of a “mythological creature” was expanded to also include the folkloric legends of other cultures. What all these creatures share is that they are not very credible, featuring fantastical characteristics that make believing in their existence both hard and tempting[^7].

But what makes something credible or not? You are likely to believe me if I told you there is a species of purple lizard that lives in South America, but most people would be sceptical of the existence of a spiky lactating mammal with a four-headed penis. Yet, there are no purple lizards, while echidnas are very much a thing[^8]. The world is full of animals that challenge our perception of what is possible and what isn’t. So much so that real animals being labelled as myths was relatively common in the past. During the colonisation of the Americas, Africa, and Asia, Europeans encountered many descriptions of fantastic animals they dismissed as myths. One such animal was the platypus, a relative of the aforementioned echidna. When a platypus pelt reached Britain for the first time, British naturalist George Shaw was convinced the creature was not real[^9]. It was only after more specimens reached the island that the British became convinced of the animal’s existence. The scepticism of Europeans from this period reveals something fundamental about our perception of credibility: that it is empirical. To the indigenous people of Australia, the platypus was nothing but a fact, an animal as real as a bird or a fish, though perhaps they too would have been surprised when they first saw the British riding horses. What makes something credible is precedent; what makes something incredible is novelty.

Perhaps our scepticism of novelty comes from the fact that we understand little about its evolution. Though we know a lot about how the characteristics of individuals evolve — how height, blood type, etc. change in a population over time — we generally have a much harder time explaining how these characteristics come to be in the first place: how do we grow, how did blood cells evolve? To make it even more complicated, when some characteristics appear they can modify the evolutionary process itself, triggering what we call major transitions in evolution. These include things like the emergence of the first cells from single replicator molecules, the acquisition of a nucleus and mitochondria, and the formation of multicellular organisms from single cells, among others[^10]. In simple terms, natural selection operates at the level of the survival of organisms: those that reproduce pass along their genes; those that die do not. However, when a major transition occurs, it fundamentally changes how evolution works by redefining what an organism is in the first place. An illustrative anecdote: two scientist bacteria are in a meeting room having a conversation; they are discussing the possibility that somewhere in the world there are beings made out of trillions of cells that together behave like a single organism, each cell concerned not with its own survival, but with that of the whole group. The bacteria laugh it off; nothing in their theories seems to indicate such a thing could evolve. The punchline? Their meeting is taking place in your gut[^11].

The problem with theories that try to predict major evolutionary transitions is that they must describe life on at least two levels, before and after the transition, while also mapping one onto the other. To see why this is hard, consider multicellularity. An ideal theory of its evolution would specify which changes in cell traits a multicellular organism needs in order to arise. The difficulty is that a multicellular organism is not simply a bunch of cells stuck together. It is something more than that, more than just the sum of its parts. Suppose we build a computer model in which cells can share resources with their neighbours. Nothing in the model suggests that cooperation should evolve: cooperators reproduce more slowly because they give resources away, while non-cooperators exploit their generosity and outgrow them. We run the simulations anyway and... voilà: highly cooperative cells evolve[^12]. Our prediction failed because of an effect we could not foresee: cooperators clustered into colonies and spontaneously began reproducing as a group, outgrowing non-cooperators. We already had the tools to describe the evolutionary dynamics of individual cells. Had we known that colonies would form, we could have used the same tools to investigate their evolution. What we missed was that the evolution of cooperation could carry us from one level to the other.

Naive evolutionary theory often falls short of predicting outcomes because biological novelty is generated from complex interactions between pre-existing parts, and we currently cannot reliably anticipate when these interactions will emerge. Explaining how novelty evolves is just one step towards answering a question as old as humanity itself: why do we exist? Evolutionary biology still has much work to do before it can claim an answer. Until then, we remain a lot like the mythological creatures of our legends: surprising, incredible, unexplainable.

[^1]: After the collapse of the Bronze Age in Greece, most settlements (with the notable exceptions of Athens and Thebes) were abandoned, and [people moved to new locations](https://ancient-greece.org/history/history-of-greece-the-dark-ages/).

[^2]: Iron was first smelted in a type of furnace called a “[bloomery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomery)”.

[^3]: The first writing system used by the Mycenaeans is called “Linear B”. It was invented in Crete, where writing had been in use since [around 2100 BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretan_hieroglyphs#CITEREFYule1981). It took about 600 years for writing to be imported from Crete to the mainland!

[^4]: Though the Odyssey is presented as a single piece, [historians believe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey) that Homer likely combined several different stories that were popular in his time. These stories were already sung by [bards](https://www.britannica.com/art/aoidos) at the time, so they might already have had the metrical structure of a [dactylic hexameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dactylic_hexameter).

[^5]: Oddly, one of the most thorough lists of mythological creatures I have ever encountered is in The Name of the Rose, a murder-mystery that features 3-4 continuous pages containing exclusively comma-separated names of Greek mythological creatures. I wish I could tell you how that was relevant to the story, but that is the one mystery [William of Baskerville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Baskerville) could not solve.

[^6]: Not quite: at the time the question was also largely “what does it mean to be Greek?”. The two questions overlap somewhat due to the Greek obsession with their perceived cultural superiority over non-Greeks.

[^7]: There is a pseudoscience called [cryptozoology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptozoology), where people go into the jungle looking for mythological or extinct creatures. Public fascination with this subject is reflected in the immense popularity of cryptozoology shows such as [Extinct or Alive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinct_or_Alive), presented by Forrest Galante. The show has actually been involved in rediscovering some species that were presumed to be extinct, though it has also been criticised for its sensationalism and for erasing the contribution of scientists and locals to the discoveries.

[^8]: The echidna easily takes the award for the most unique reproductive system in the animal kingdom: not only does its penis have four heads, but its spermatozoids bundle up in clusters of about 100 that migrate collectively! Graphical but interesting blog post [here](https://www.livescience.com/mystery-of-4-headed-echidna-penis-solved.html).

[^9]: Thankfully, [he decided to give it a scientific name](https://hoaxes.org/archive/permalink/the_duckbilled_platypus) just to be safe: *Platypus anatinus*, or flat-footed duck.

[^10]: The original concept of what constitutes a major transition in evolution was devised by Maynard Smith and Szathmáry in their [namesake book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Major_Transitions_in_Evolution), which included a list of eight transitions. Since then, people have argued about what should and should not be in the list, and have also come up with related terms such as “transitions in biological complexity”.

[^11]: We have about [30 trillion bacteria](https://bluehorizonbloodtests.co.uk/blogs/gut-health/how-many-bacteria-are-in-the-human-gut) living in our gut, approximately the same number of cells as in the human body.

[^12]: This work was done by colleagues at the University of Utrecht. [Here is the paper](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1010612).
